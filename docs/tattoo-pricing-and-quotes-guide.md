# Tattoo Pricing and Quote Requests: A Guide for Tattoo Artists

"How much for this?" is the most common message in a tattoo artist's inbox, and the hardest to answer in one line. Quote blind and you undercharge or scare people off. Ignore it and you lose the booking. Publish a rigid price list and clients hold you to a number that never fit their piece. This guide gives you a tool-neutral way to handle pricing and quote requests: what to share publicly, what to ask before quoting, and how to reply so the conversation moves toward a booking instead of a haggle.

It pairs with the [booking form fields](tattoo-booking-form-fields.md), the details you need to price a piece, and the [deposit policy template](tattoo-deposit-policy-template.md), what happens once a price is agreed.

## Why tattoo pricing is hard to quote up front

A tattoo is not a fixed product. The same idea costs different amounts depending on:

- Size and placement (ribs and hands take longer than a forearm)
- Detail and style (fine-line micro-realism versus bold traditional)
- Color versus black and grey
- The number of sessions a piece realistically needs
- Your experience, your demand, and your region

A client who asks for "a small rose, how much?" has not given you enough to price honestly. That is not their fault. It is why a quote request needs structure, the same way a booking request does.

## What to share publicly versus in a quote

Publishing some pricing context filters tire-kickers and sets expectations before anyone messages you. Keep it to ranges and rules, not exact per-piece prices.

| Share publicly | Keep for the quote |
|---|---|
| Your shop minimum | The exact price for a specific piece |
| Your hourly or day rate | The total for a multi-session project |
| Deposit amount or percentage | The final number after seeing references |
| Whether color costs more than black and grey | Any negotiated adjustments |
| That custom work is quoted per piece | The scope you propose for their budget |

A public minimum and rate answer most "is this artist in my budget?" questions without a single DM. The specific number waits until you have seen the idea.

## Common tattoo pricing models

| Model | How it works | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Shop minimum | A floor price for any tattoo, however small | Everyone; covers setup and supplies |
| Hourly rate | Price equals hours in the chair times your rate | Custom or freehand work of uncertain length |
| Day rate | A flat price for a full session day, often discounted versus hourly | Large pieces, sleeves, back pieces, guest spots |
| Piece rate (flat quote) | One agreed price for the whole tattoo | Flash, repeat designs, well-defined pieces |
| Flash fixed price | A pre-set price per design, sometimes by size tier | Flash days and walk-ins |

Most artists mix these: a minimum, plus an hourly or day rate for custom work, plus fixed prices for flash. State which one applies when you quote so the client understands what the number means.

## What a quote request needs from the client

You cannot price what you cannot see. Ask for the same core details as a booking request, because a quote and a booking start from the same information.

| Field | Why it affects price |
|---|---|
| Tattoo idea (in their words) | Scope and complexity |
| Placement | Time, difficulty, stencil work |
| Approximate size | The single biggest price driver |
| Color or black and grey | Color usually adds time |
| Reference images | Detail level you cannot guess from text |
| Budget range (optional) | Lets you propose a fitting scope |
| Single session or open to multiple | Whether you quote a piece or a day rate |

If a client sends only "how much for a sleeve?", reply with the request fields rather than a number. See the [client intake template](../templates/tattoo-client-intake-template.md) for a form that collects all of this at once.

## A quote you can copy

When you have the details, a clear quote does three things: it gives a number or range, says what it covers, and states the next step. For example:

> Thanks for the references. For a black and grey forearm piece this size, I work at a day rate of [insert rate], and a piece like this usually fits in one session. To book, I take a [insert amount or percentage] deposit that comes off the final cost. Want me to send the booking form?

For uncertain pieces, quote a range and explain it:

> Hard to give an exact number before I draw it, but a half sleeve like this usually runs [insert range] across [insert number] sessions. I will confirm once the design is ready. The deposit to start is [insert amount].

Keep the wording yours. The structure, a number or range, what it covers, and the next step, is what keeps a quote from turning into a negotiation.

## Common mistakes

- **Quoting blind.** A price without references or size is a guess you will be held to. Ask first.
- **Giving an exact price before the design exists.** Custom work changes as you draw it. Quote a range and confirm at design approval.
- **Negotiating down in the DMs.** Discounting on request trains clients to haggle and undervalues your time. Hold your minimum and rate.
- **Hiding pricing entirely.** Zero public context means every casual browser becomes a DM you have to answer. A minimum and a rate filter for you.
- **Quoting in a channel you will lose.** A number agreed in a DM thread vanishes. Move agreed pricing into the booking request and the deposit confirmation.
- **Forgetting the deposit is part of the price.** Say that the deposit comes off the final cost, or clients read it as an extra fee.

## Pricing and quote checklist

- [ ] Your shop minimum is stated publicly
- [ ] Your rate model (hourly, day rate, or piece) is clear
- [ ] Quote requests collect idea, placement, size, color, and references
- [ ] You quote a range for custom work and a fixed price for flash
- [ ] Every quote says what it covers and the next step
- [ ] The deposit is described as applied to the final cost, not an extra
- [ ] Agreed pricing is recorded in the booking request, not left in a DM
