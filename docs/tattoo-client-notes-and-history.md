# Keeping Tattoo Client Notes and History

Good client notes turn a stranger's second booking into a returning client you already understand. They are the private record you keep alongside the booking: what was tattooed, how it healed, what the client is sensitive to, what is planned next, and the small preferences that make the next session smoother. A booking form captures one request. Client notes capture everything that happens after it, across every session and every year the client keeps coming back.

This is your record, not the client's. Keep it wherever you already trust: a notes app, a spreadsheet, a dedicated tool. What matters is that it stays consistent, searchable, and honest about the difference between a fact and a guess.

## What should a tattoo artist keep in client notes?

Enough to pick up exactly where you left off, and nothing you would not want to be responsible for storing. A practical core:

- Who they are: name or preferred handle, the contact you actually reach them on, and their Instagram handle if that is how they found you.
- Session log: date, what was tattooed, placement, size, and how long it took. One line per session is plenty.
- Health flags that carry across sessions: anything relevant to healing or safety the client raised at intake, such as skin conditions, allergies, fainting, or medication that affects bleeding. Collect these properly at booking (see the consent and health intake guide); the notes are where the ones that still matter live afterward.
- Healing and touch-ups: how the piece settled, any rework done, and the touch-up window you agreed.
- Preferences: how they sit, pain tolerance, breaks, numbing, whether they like to talk or zone out. Small things that make a long session easier.
- Money record: deposits taken, what was paid, and anything still owed, so nobody reconstructs it from memory.
- Communication notes: what you last told them, and what you are waiting on.

## What belongs in client history versus the booking request?

The booking request is one moment: the idea, references, and details a client sent for a single piece. Client history is the running total across every request, session, and message. A returning client sends a fresh request each time; their history is what tells you it is the same person, what you have already done for them, and what you learned last time.

Keep the two linked but separate. The request stays as the client submitted it (see the booking form fields guide). The history is yours to write, and it grows.

## How to organize notes so they stay useful

- One record per client, not per booking. Everything about that person lives in one place, so their third tattoo can see the notes from their first.
- Make it searchable by the thing you actually remember, which is usually their handle or email, rarely their legal name.
- Keep private notes clearly private. A note like "books, then reschedules once, every time" is for you and never for a client-facing screen. If your tool shows any field to clients, keep your candid notes out of it.
- Put a date on everything. A note with no date is a guess in six months.
- Log the session on the day, not the week after. Ten seconds while it is fresh beats a blank you cannot fill later.

## Notes for multi-session and returning clients

Long projects are where notes earn their place. A backpiece over five sittings is five entries: what was outlined, what was filled, what is left, how each area healed, and where you stopped. Without that log you reopen the project from memory, and the client notices.

For a returning client, a quick scan of their history before they arrive is the whole trick. You already know the placement quirks, the pace they like, and the healed result you are matching. It reads as care because it is care.

## Privacy: what not to write, and why

Client notes are personal data, and some of it, health information especially, is sensitive. Treat the record accordingly:

- Keep only what you use. A reason-to-know test beats hoarding.
- Do not store more health detail than a session needs, and keep it apart from casual notes.
- Photos of healing or of a client's body are records too. Store them with the same care, and get consent before using any of them publicly.
- Assume the record could be read by someone else one day, and write notes you would stand behind.
- Check your local rules on storing and deleting client and health data. This is a workflow guide, not legal advice.

## A simple client record

Copy this per client into whatever you use, and add sessions as they happen.

---

**Client:** ___ (handle: ___, email: ___)

**First booked:** ___

**Health flags that still matter:** ___

**Preferences:** ___ (pace, breaks, numbing, seating)

**Deposit and payment record:** ___

**Sessions**

- ___ (date) | placement: ___ | what was done: ___ | time: ___ | healing and notes: ___
- ___
- ___

**Planned next:** ___

**Private notes (never client-facing):** ___

---

## Common mistakes

- Keeping notes per booking instead of per client, so a returning client's history scatters across separate forms.
- Writing candid notes into a field the client can see.
- Storing sensitive health detail you never actually use.
- Never dating entries, so the record cannot be trusted later.
- Trusting memory for money, then disagreeing with a client about what was paid.

## Client notes checklist

- [ ] One record per client, not per booking
- [ ] Searchable by handle or email
- [ ] Every session logged with a date
- [ ] Health flags that matter carried from intake, kept separate from casual notes
- [ ] Private notes never shown on a client-facing screen
- [ ] Deposits and payments recorded, not remembered
- [ ] Only the client and health data you actually use is stored
